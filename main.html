Charlie and the
Chocolate Factory

Roald Dahl

A fever dream of sweet and whimsy

Right off the bat, The Chocolate Factory has two main selling points: the strange but creative sweets and the larger-than-life owner of the factory, Willy Wonka. Willy Wonka remains one of the greatest tourguides the world has seen, and the only capitalist the world will celebrate. Wonka is simultaneously charismatic, mysterious, and eating-off-the-ground dangerous. I know the film adaptations had incredible (yes, I said that) Wonka representations (including Timothee Chalamet), but I still love the Book Wonka. There is kindness and love in Book Wonka that the Film Wonkas could not replicate. Book Wonka was a nerd, an inventor, a child in his own room showing his parents all the cool stuff he made. Granted the things were erratic and can maim children, but still Book Wonka was a fun companion due to his charming personality. Ultimately, Book Wonka felt…kind. The Film Wonkas were sarcastic, and almost antagonistic towards children. The Film Wonkas revelled in the disappearance of the children from the tour.

Book Wonka was innocent. Strange, but innocent (I say autism but I’m not 100% sure). One throwaway line that I appreciate in the book was when Grandpa Joe was telling Wonka’s story to Charlie for the first time. He said that when Wonka closed down the factory, he said sorry to his employees and let them all go. That line itself, although inocuous and possibly filtered through Grandpa Joe’s nostalgia, felt warm for me. That contradicted with the cold, snappy personality I’ve come to associate with Wonka due to the films.

Charlie and the lack of flavor

What pulled the book down? Well, Charlie. Yes, the Charlie. The Charlie who ended up owning the factory. He’s just not a good character. Yes, Charlie was supposed to be a blank slate, a receptacle for all things good and virtuous. Charlie was a role model. When children read The Chocolate Factory, they were meant to say, “If I get the chance to be in the Chocolate Factory, I would be like Charlie.” and for good reason. After all, Wonka made the tour a test, so he can assess the children and choose his successor and if kids want to win, they should be like Charlie.

My problem with Charlie is that it felt as if he did nothing to earn the factory. Wonka had a whole spiel by the end of the book how he didn’t want an adult to take over the factory, and that only a child filled with love and kindness can see his vision blah blah blah. That’s simple enough to understand until you realize that Charlie didn’t do anything that greatly exhibited these traits in the book. Sure, he avoided the “traps” and “temptations” that the other children fell for but that showed passiveness (if anything) and not kindness. Charlie won by default which made it impossible to join the Charlie train. It would have been better if Charlie, through a grand gesture, showed good character. For instance, Charlie chould have refused the factory, accepted just the food, and asked for a new house while they were at it. It could have been a silver axe - gold axe situation, and I would have been satisfied.

The Chocolate Factory had amazing worldbuilding (or factorybuilding wink), a wacky set of children characters, and an erratic tour guide with more time than he knows what to do with. Ultimately, the lines and sweets were memorable. The book is funny, but as the plot gains traction by sacrificing the blood of not-so-innocent children, it screeches into a halt due to a Charlie-shaped hole in its chassis (I don’t know cars). That said, the book remains one of the lovely classics out there that not only will be part of pop culture but also stand the test of time as a great example of science fiction in children’s literature.

** Roald Dahl is a controversial author, his family has acknowledged his issues, and despite him still being part of the lexicon for children literature, I do not condone his ideas nor his character designs. I hate Charlie.
